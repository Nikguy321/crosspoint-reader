# Where to look

Look and listen before walking far. Moving costs water.

- **Low ground.** Valley bottoms, gullies and the foot of slopes and cliffs.
- **Greener plants.** A line or patch of thicker, brighter green plants, or leafy trees among dry brush or conifers.
- **Animal trails.** Trails that join and head downhill often lead to water. More joins means you are getting closer.
- **Birds.** Doves and pigeons fly low and straight toward water in the early morning and evening.
- **Insects.** Swarms of mosquitoes and flies usually mean water is close.
- **Rock cracks.** Bird droppings around a crack can mark water trapped inside.
- **Sound.** Listen for running water at dawn and dusk, when it is quiet.

Choose flowing water over still, stagnant water. Treat all water you find; see **Making water safe**.

WARNING: Stay off ice you have not checked, and back from undercut banks and fast water. No ice is 100% safe.

NOTE: If you are lost, staying put is usually safer than wandering far in search of water. See EMERGENCY.

# Dig for water
![Where to dig: outside bends and green spots](atp-p66-2)

No surface water? Dig where there are signs:

- the outside bend of a dry streambed
- the foot of a cliff or rock outcrop
- greener plants in a low spot
- damp sand

Dig until the soil turns damp, then a little deeper. Wait for water to seep in. Scoop gently; treat it.

WARNING: A dry streambed can flood in minutes from rain you cannot see. Never camp in one.

# Beach wells
![Beach well behind the first dune](atp-p66-1)

On a sea beach, dig behind the first dune, above the high-tide line, 3 to 5 feet (1 to 1.5 m) down. Line the sides with driftwood, the bottom with stones. It can take hours to fill.

Salty? Do not drink it; dig farther inland. Always treat it.

WARNING: Sand walls cave in. Never climb into a deep hole, and keep children out.
