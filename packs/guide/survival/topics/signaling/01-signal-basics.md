# Be seen

Searchers look from the air and on foot. Make yourself easy to spot.

- **Get into the open.** Use the biggest clear, flat area you can reach safely, on the highest ground nearby: a meadow, a ridge, a gravel bar, a lakeshore. Stay out of shadows and away from thick trees. Leave ridges when thunder is near.
- **Make shapes nature does not.** Straight lines, square corners, circles, triangles and big letters catch a searcher's eye.
- **Use contrast.** Dark on snow. Bright colors on green or brown. Piles of rocks or brush cast shadows that show from above.
- **Add movement and light.** A waving flag, a mirror flash, a fire or a strobe draws the eye.
- **Three of anything means distress:** three fires, three whistle blasts, three flashes, three rock piles.
- **Wear bright clothing** and spread bright gear out in the open.

NOTE: Once your signals are set up, stay with them. A person who keeps moving is much harder to find.

# Be ready

An aircraft may be in view for only moments. Have your signals ready before you hear it.

- **Keep a whistle, a mirror and a light on your body**, on a cord or in a pocket, not deep in your pack.
- **Build signals now.** Lay out a ground sign. Stack fire materials and keep them dry and covered, ready to light.
- **Use more than one kind of signal**: a ground sign and a mirror by day, a fire and a light at night.
- **Plan who does what** the moment someone hears an engine or rotor.
- **Save batteries.** Use a light when you see or hear searchers, not all night.
- **Keep signaling** until rescuers reach you or tell you to stop. A passing aircraft may not have seen you.

Practice before your trip: aim a mirror, blow your whistle, find your phone's flashlight in the dark. Skills you have tried work better under stress.
