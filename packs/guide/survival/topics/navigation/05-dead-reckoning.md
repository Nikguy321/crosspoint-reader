# Steering marks

1. Take a bearing toward where you want to go.
2. Pick a **steering mark** on that line: the farthest thing you can still recognize as you get close. A tall, odd-shaped tree, a boulder or a notch on the skyline works.
3. Put the compass away and walk to it, going around whatever is in the way.
4. At the mark, take the bearing again and pick the next mark.

**Nothing ahead to use?** Look back along the reverse bearing to a mark behind you, and keep it straight behind you.

**Going around an obstacle:** turn 90 degrees and count paces until you are clear. Turn back to your bearing and walk past it (these paces count toward your distance). Turn 90 degrees toward your line, walk the same count back, then turn onto your bearing again.

**If you must move at night,** steer by a star low in the sky ahead. Stars move: recheck with the compass every 15 minutes or so.
+++
# Use the land to guide you

**Handrail:** a long feature that runs the way you are going, such as a stream, a ridge, a trail or a power line. Follow it instead of steering by compass.

**Catching feature:** something you cannot miss that tells you to turn, or that you went too far. "If I reach the big stream, I have gone past camp."

**Aiming off:** when your goal is on a long feature, like a bridge on a stream, aim about 10 degrees to one side on purpose. When you reach the stream, you know which way to turn. Each degree off moves you about 17 feet for every 1,000 feet you walk (5 m per 300 m).

**Attack point:** an easy-to-find spot close to your goal, ideally within about 500 yards (450 m). Walk there the easy way, then go carefully by bearing and pace count.

Small errors add up. Each time you reach a feature you can find on the map, check where you are and start your count again.
+++
# Count your paces

A pace here is two steps: count each time your **left foot** lands.

**Learn your count.** Walk a measured 100 yards (or 100 m) on flat ground several times and average the counts. Write the number on your compass or map.

**Keep track** of each 100: move a pebble from one pocket to another, or tie a knot in a cord.

**Your count goes up** (shorter paces) when you walk:

- uphill
- in snow, mud, sand or loose gravel
- through brush or deadfall
- into a strong wind
- with a heavy pack, or tired
- in the dark

On gentle downhills, paces get longer and the count goes down. On steep ones they shorten again.

Pace counts are rough. Use them to know roughly how far you have gone, then confirm with the map.

NOTE: Write your counts down as you go. Under stress, numbers are easy to lose.
