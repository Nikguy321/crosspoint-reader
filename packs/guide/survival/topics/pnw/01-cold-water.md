# Cold water shock

Rivers, lakes and the sea here stay cold all year. Water below 70 F (21 C) can cause cold shock, as hard at 50 to 60 F (10 to 15 C) as near freezing.

**When you fall in:**

- You gasp without meaning to. If your face is under, you breathe in water.
- Fast, panicked breathing follows for the first 2 to 3 minutes or more.
- This can drown you long before your body gets cold.

**What to do first:**

1. Keep your head up and your mouth clear of the water.
2. Do not thrash or swim yet. Hold anything that floats.
3. Work on slowing your breathing. It settles in a few minutes.
4. Then make a plan to get out.

NOTE: A life jacket holds your head up through the gasp. Wear one on and near cold water.

NOTE: Reference only - not a substitute for first-aid training. Call for help first.

# Self-rescue

Cold water drains strength from your hands and arms fast. Without a life jacket you may have **about 10 minutes** of useful movement, often less.

1. Once your breathing settles, find the closest way out.
2. **If you can reach safety, go now,** calmly: onto the bank, a boat or a big floating log.
3. **If you cannot, do not swim for a far shore.** Save your heat and strength and wait for rescue.
4. Get as much of your body out of the water as you can, onto anything that floats.
5. In a life jacket, float still: knees drawn up to your chin, legs together, arms pressed to your sides, head out of the water.
6. With others, huddle close together to share heat.
7. Blow a whistle or wave to signal for help.

NOTE: Reference only - not a substitute for first-aid training. Call for help first.

+++

# Through ice, and after

**If you fall through ice:**

1. Turn back toward the way you came. That ice held you.
2. Put your hands and arms flat on the unbroken ice.
3. Kick your legs hard to slide forward onto the ice on your belly.
4. **Roll away from the hole.** Do not stand up.
5. Crawl back along your tracks until you reach solid ice.

To help someone else, stay off the ice. Reach or throw a rope or branch from the shore.

**After any cold-water rescue,** treat for hypothermia even if the person seems fine:

- Handle them gently and keep them lying down.
- Get them out of wet clothes and into dry insulation.
- Warm the trunk first, not the hands and feet. Never put them in a warm bath.

See FIRST AID, Hypothermia and frostbite.

# Wet cold

Cool, windy rain is common here and is dangerous. Hypothermia can happen **above 40 F (4 C)** when you are wet from rain, sweat or a fall in water.

- Wear wool or synthetic layers. Cotton and jeans stay wet and cold.
- Put rain gear on **before** you get wet.
- Avoid sweating: shed a layer or open vents when you climb.
- Eat and drink often. Food is fuel for body heat.
- When you stop, swap wet layers for dry ones. Keep a dry set sealed in a plastic bag.
- **Stop and make shelter early,** while your hands still work. Do not push on into the dark cold and wet.
- Watch each other for warning signs: hard shivering, slurred speech, fumbling hands, stumbling, drowsiness.

For treatment, see FIRST AID, Hypothermia and frostbite.

NOTE: Reference only - not a substitute for first-aid training. Call for help first.
